# Deep Research Request #2 — More Quantifiable Value Levers

## Context
I'm extending the value/ROI section of a stakeholder pitch for a demand-forecasting and
inventory-reordering software tool for small-to-mid-size DTC / Shopify retailers ($500K–$5M
revenue). The tool forecasts demand per product, sizes safety stock, tells the merchant when and
how much to reorder, and flags dead/slow-moving stock.

I already have a strong, well-sourced lever for **stockouts**: retailers lose ~4% of sales to
stockouts (Corsten & Gruen), and 47% of stockouts are caused by store ordering & forecasting
decisions (Gruen, Corsten & Bharadwaj 2002). I want to find equally defensible numbers for the
tool's OTHER functions.

## Source-quality bar — STRICT
**ACCEPT only:** peer-reviewed academic journals (Journal of Retailing, Int'l Journal of
Production Economics, Production & Operations Management, Management Science, EJOR); the original
Gruen/Corsten studies; large industry-analyst studies with transparent methodology (e.g., IHL
Group); academic supply-chain textbooks; or government/standards sources.
**REJECT:** vendor and SaaS marketing blogs, consulting content marketing, self-attributed claims,
listicles, "research institutes" with no verifiable existence, and any statistic with no
traceable primary source.
**If you cannot find a credible source, say so — do NOT substitute a weaker one.**

## For every finding, report:
- The exact figure + a **verbatim quote**
- Full citation (author, publication, year, link)
- Population/sample (sector, region, company size)
- The **baseline** it's measured against
- A one-line note on source strength

---

## Questions, in priority order

### 1. Causes of overstock / excess inventory (HIGHEST PRIORITY)
Is there an authoritative breakdown of the **causes of retail overstock / excess inventory** —
specifically, what share is attributable to **over-forecasting, poor demand planning, or
over-ordering (in-house decisions)** versus other causes (supplier minimum-order quantities,
returns, cancellations, supply-chain timing)? I want to do for overstock exactly what
Gruen/Corsten did for stockouts: attribute a defensible % of the loss to the ordering/forecasting
decisions a tool can fix. Provide the breakdown with exact figures and quotes.

### 2. Consumer response to stockouts — lost customer value
From Gruen & Corsten's out-of-stock consumer-response research (and peer-reviewed corroboration):
when a shopper encounters a stockout, what percentage **buy the item elsewhere, substitute/switch
brand, delay the purchase, or abandon it entirely**? And is there any figure for the **long-term
or repeat-customer value lost** when a shopper permanently defects to a competitor after a
stockout? Goal: show that a stockout can cost more than a single lost sale.

### 3. Retail markdown rates and their causes
What percentage of retail sales is lost to **markdowns / discounting** (ideally by segment —
apparel, general merchandise, DTC/e-commerce)? And what share of markdowns is attributable to
**over-buying / excess inventory / poor demand planning** versus planned promotional markdowns?
Peer-reviewed or authoritative industry sources.

### 4. Inventory carrying cost rate
What is the widely-accepted **annual inventory carrying cost as a % of inventory value**, from an
authoritative source (academic supply-chain texts, APQC, industry standards bodies)? Break down
the components (cost of capital, storage, insurance, obsolescence/shrink) if available.

### 5. (Optional) Working capital tied up in inventory — SMB / DTC
Average **inventory-to-sales ratio, or inventory as a % of annual revenue**, for small/mid-size
retail or DTC e-commerce, from a credible source — so I can estimate the cash tied up in
inventory. Note the population.

---

## Deliverable
A structured summary by the five questions, with per-finding details. Where a well-sourced figure
genuinely does not exist, state that clearly.

## What I most want
**Question 1 is the priority.** If an authoritative causes-of-overstock breakdown exists, it lets
me say "X% of overstock is an over-ordering / demand-planning problem — the exact thing this tool
fixes," mirroring my strongest existing stockout claim. Question 2 is second: it would show a
stockout costs more than one sale, from the same gold-standard source I already cite.
