# Deep Research Request — Evidence for an Inventory Tool's Value Case

## Context
I'm building the value/ROI section of a stakeholder pitch for a demand-forecasting and
inventory-reordering software tool aimed at small-to-mid-size direct-to-consumer / Shopify
retailers (roughly $500K–$5M annual revenue). The tool forecasts demand per product, sizes
safety stock at a chosen service level, and tells the merchant when and how much to reorder.
Its real competitor is a spreadsheet or manual judgment — not enterprise software.

I need rigorously sourced evidence to quantify (a) what poor inventory management costs
retailers and (b) how much of that is recoverable by adopting systematic, forecast-driven
reordering. I have already been burned by weak citations, so **source quality is the priority.**

## Source-quality bar — STRICT
**ACCEPT only:** peer-reviewed academic journals (e.g., *Journal of Retailing*, *International
Journal of Production Economics*, *Production and Operations Management*, *Management Science*,
*European Journal of Operational Research*); the original Gruen & Corsten out-of-stock studies;
large industry-analyst studies with transparent methodology (e.g., IHL Group); or
government/standards sources.

**REJECT:** vendor and SaaS marketing blogs, consulting-firm content marketing, self-attributed
claims ("after helping 200+ clients"), listicles, and any statistic with no traceable primary
source.

**If you cannot find a credible source for something, say so explicitly — do NOT substitute a
weaker source to fill the gap.**

## For every finding, report:
- The exact figure
- A **verbatim quote** containing it
- Full citation: author(s), publication, year, and a link
- The population/sample studied (sector, region, company size)
- **The baseline it is measured against** (critical)
- A one-line note on source strength

---

## Research questions, in priority order

### 1. Causes of retail stockouts (HIGHEST PRIORITY)
In Gruen, Corsten & Bharadwaj's worldwide out-of-stock research (and any peer-reviewed
corroboration), **what share of retail out-of-stocks is caused by store-level ordering,
forecasting, and replenishment decisions versus upstream supply-chain / distribution causes?**
I'm looking for the specific breakdown (e.g., "X% of stockouts are due to store ordering and
forecasting practices"). Provide the exact percentages and verbatim quotes.

### 2. Recovery — reduction in stockouts AND in excess inventory from systematic forecasting
Find peer-reviewed studies quantifying:
- (a) the reduction in **stockout rate or lost sales**, and
- (b) the reduction in **excess/obsolete inventory or markdowns**,

when a retailer moves from naive/manual forecasting (moving average, judgment) to systematic
statistical or machine-learning demand planning with safety-stock / reorder policies.

**Constraints:** the metric must be *stockout/lost-sales reduction* or *excess-inventory/markdown
reduction* — **NOT** inventory carrying-cost %, and **NOT** forecast-accuracy / MAPE improvement.
The baseline should be **naive/manual** where possible. Prefer e-commerce / DTC / SMB
populations; state the population for each figure.

### 3. Segment-matched loss figures
- (a) **Stockout / lost-sales as a % of revenue for e-commerce or DTC retailers specifically**
  (to complement the grocery/FMCG Gruen-Corsten ~4% figure, which may not fit a Shopify merchant).
- (b) A **peer-reviewed or primary-source figure for overstock / excess-inventory cost as a % of
  revenue** (to corroborate IHL Group's industry estimate of ~3.2%).

### 4. The value of safety-stock / reorder-point policies
Operations-research or inventory-management evidence that implementing **service-level-based
safety stock and reorder-point policies measurably reduces stockouts** compared with reactive or
manual reordering. (Supports the claim that the win is the reorder *discipline*, not the model.)

### 5. (Optional) Statistical/ML vs naive forecasting
Academic comparisons (e.g., the **M5 forecasting competition**) showing how much statistical or ML
methods reduce forecast error versus a **naive moving-average baseline** in retail demand.

### 6. (Optional) Time spent on manual inventory management
Any credible **survey** (not vendor marketing) reporting hours per week that SMB or e-commerce
retailers spend on manual inventory management, reordering, or demand planning.

---

## Deliverable
A structured summary organized by the six questions above, with the per-finding details listed.
Where a well-sourced figure genuinely does not exist, **state that clearly** so I don't rely on a
weak one.

## What I'll do with it
Question 1 is the one I most want to land: if the "share of stockouts caused by ordering/
replenishment decisions" figure holds up in the original source, it lets me say "the majority of
stockouts are an ordering problem — exactly what this tool fixes," cited to a gold-standard
academic study, instead of relying on a soft vendor "recovery %."
